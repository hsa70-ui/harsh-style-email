# Write Email in Harsh's Style

Use a courteous, measured voice with an early purpose and a clear next step. The profile comes from deidentified English email patterns. Use the current sender's own name, role, account and facts. Follow any explicit tone or length request from the sender.

## Choose the shape

- **Formal first contact.** Use `Dear [Name or established title],`. A short courtesy line is optional. In the next sentence or paragraph, state the purpose. Add enough context for the reader to decide, then make the ask specific. Close with `Best regards,` and the sender's full name. `Dear Professor [Surname],` works for faculty when the title and surname are known.
- **Formal submission or practical request.** Use a short paragraph to say what you are sending or asking for. Name a confirmed file or item and include the requested action or timing if known. `Regards,` is a natural close. `Warm regards,` suits a warmer established exchange.
- **Ongoing exchange.** Open with the answer, a specific thank-you, or the next action. Use `Hi [First name],` if the thread already supports a familiar greeting. A fast exchange can omit a repeated greeting or sign-off. For a reply with several points, preserve the necessary conditions, options and commitments in short paragraphs. Keep a simple confirmation brief.
- **Follow-up.** Refer to the earlier request or item in one sentence. State the exact update you need and the relevant deadline, if there is one. Keep the courtesy low-key and avoid restating the full history.

For a new email, put the topic and purpose in a concrete subject, often four to ten words. Keep the thread subject when replying. As a starting point, substantive new emails often need three or four short paragraphs. Routine replies often fit in one or two. Use a short list if the recipient needs to check several separate items.

## Language and facts

- Use one request phrase that fits the situation, such as `I wanted to ask if [request]`, `Could you please [action]?` or `Please let me know [specific question]`. Use `I am happy to [action]` when the sender has offered it. `Thank you for [specific help]` gives the closing a reason.
- Use full verb forms in formal mail and natural contractions in familiar threads. Use restrained punctuation. Keep warm language specific to the occasion.
- Use titles already known from the request or thread. Mention an attachment, deadline, availability or completed action only when the facts establish it. When a decision or fact is missing, ask for it if needed to complete the draft.
- Return the subject and body in the host's email format when available. Preserve the sender's actual identity. A drafting request produces a draft for review. A separate instruction is needed to send it.

## Synthetic examples

**New request**

Subject: Request for feedback on the workshop outline

Dear Professor [Surname],

I wanted to ask if you could review the outline for the workshop. I have separated the practical exercise from the discussion and would appreciate your feedback on whether the exercise leaves enough time for questions.

If you have a chance to review it this week, please let me know if you would suggest any changes.

Best regards,  
[Sender name]

**Submission with a confirmed attachment**

Subject: Updated agenda for the planning meeting

Dear [Name],

Please find attached the updated agenda for the planning meeting. I have included the revised discussion order and the two items that need a decision.

Please let me know if there is anything else you would like me to include.

Regards,  
[Sender name]

**Short reply in an established exchange**

Hi [First name],

Thank you for confirming. Thursday at 2 p.m. works for me.

Best,  
[Sender first name]

The reply assumes the sender has confirmed their availability. These examples illustrate structure and tone. Use current facts and relationship cues for each new draft.
